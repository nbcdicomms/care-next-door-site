Get release updates. ABOUT Who helped raise you Maybe it was a grandmother who kept you through the summer. An auntie who knew how to settle you. An older cousin who taught you the newest dance. A neighbor who made room at the table. A family friend who became family. This is Family, Friend, and Neighbor care. It is the care that surrounds a child with people who know their rhythms, honor their identity, and help them grow. In Black communities, it is also a living tradition shaped by shared responsibility, trust, and love across generations. The Care Next Door invites us to see this care clearly, value the expertise within it, and imagine what becomes possible when the systems around families support the care they already choose. Family, Friend, and Neighbor care is child care provided by someone a parent or guardian knows and trusts: a grandparent, aunt, uncle, older sibling, cousin, godparent, close friend, or neighbor held within a family's circle of care. It can be full time or occasional. It can happen before school, after school, overnight, during the summer, in an emergency, or whenever working and caring for family must meet. For many Black families, it is a deliberate choice, offering flexibility, cultural understanding, continuity, and the deep safety of being known. 5M+ FFN caregivers nationwide 11.5M children in their care No. 1 most used form of child care in the U.S. Source: National Survey of Early Care and Education, 2019 The most common form of child care in the United States is also among the least visible in child care policy, research, and public investment. LEGACY Our care has always carried us forward Black Family, Friend, and Neighbor care reaches beyond any single home or generation. Its roots extend to communal caregiving traditions in precolonial Africa, where children were held within networks of kinship and shared responsibility. Across forced separation, migration, exclusion, and change, Black communities continued to expand the meaning of family and create places where children could belong. That legacy lives in everyday acts: teaching a child a family recipe, passing down songs and stories, tending to a child's hair, recognizing a gift before the child has words for it, and offering guidance shaped by a lifetime of knowing the family. This care is cultural infrastructure. It carries memory, identity, language, faith, foodways, play, protection, and possibility from one generation to the next. “My grandmother took care of me, and she took care of my son and daughter. Now I'm able to take care of my grandchildren and tell them how great they are and how they come from great people.” FFN CAREGIVER FEATURED IN THE DOCUMENTARY Ancestral Roots Communal caregiving and expanded kinship center children within a village of shared responsibility. Generations of Care Black families preserve safety, knowledge, culture, and connection through changing conditions. Care Today Families continue to choose trusted caregivers who know their children and respond to their individual needs. The Future We Are Building Caregivers and families shape systems that honor their expertise, autonomy, and vision. WHY THIS STORY MATTERS What we recognize shapes what we resource Families rely on FFN care every day, yet many public systems still struggle to see it. When child care is defined only through institutions and licenses, the knowledge held by trusted caregivers can disappear from view, along with the learning, safety, cultural affirmation, and stability families experience in their care. This gap has consequences. Caregivers may pay for books, toys, food, transportation, and activities from their own pockets. Families can be shut out of supports because the care they choose does not fit a narrow category. Policymakers and researchers can make decisions without hearing from the people who understand this care from the inside. The Care Next Door brings those voices to the center. The film asks us to recognize FFN caregivers as experts and to build policy, funding, and research practices that learn from them. There are no voiceless people. There are just unheard people. Documentary participant BLACK CHILDREN ARE SAFE WHEN THEY ARE FULLY SEEN Known deeply and cared for fully Black children have distinct safety needs. They deserve to experience the fullness of childhood, learn in ways that affirm who they are, receive guidance rooted in love, and build trusting relationships with adults who know them well. Existential Safety The freedom to be a child without being adultified, rushed, or treated as a threat. Learning Safety The freedom to learn, wonder, and see Black identity reflected in books, toys, stories, and possibility. Discipline Safety Guidance without harsh punishment, shaped by love and an understanding of the individual child. Interpersonal Safety Trusting relationships with caregivers who know the child, affirm their identity, and protect their belonging. In FFN care, parents often find people who know their child's history, communication, routines, triggers, strengths, and gifts. That knowledge can offer children consistency and give parents the confidence to work, rest, and meet their families' needs. “When Grandpop was able to step in, I knew they were safe. He knew the triggers. He knew my son.” PARENT OF A CHILD WITH SPECIAL NEEDS, FEATURED IN THE DOCUMENTARY INSIDE THE AFROFUTURIST EXPLORATIONS Research that makes room for memory, imagination, and truth NBCDI's Reimagining Child Care initiative brought together 51 Black FFN caregivers and parents or guardians whose children receive FFN care. In Detroit, Iowa, Nashville, and Philadelphia, participants entered spaces designed for conversation, creativity, connection, and curiosity. Guided by NBCDI's Afrofuturist Systems Design Process, the explorations began with the knowledge families already hold. Participants reflected on the care they had received, named what their communities need now, and imagined systems where Black children and caregivers can thrive. Afrofuturism is a way of imagining futures and alternative realities through a Black cultural lens. It treats Black identity, freedom, agency, and imagination as foundations for building liberated futures. Living Room Conversations Caregivers and parents spoke from experience about trust, safety, identity, isolation, joy, and the choices they make for children. Letters Across Generations Participants wrote to ancestors and future descendants, connecting gratitude and inherited wisdom to their hopes for care yet to come. Painting in Living Color Participants painted bouquets using color to express growth, dignity, peace, belonging, purpose, and joy. Affirmation Boards Images, colors, shapes, and words created space for feelings and visions that did not always fit within a conventional interview. EXPLORE THE RESEARCH WHAT CAREGIVERS AND FAMILIES SHARED A picture of care shaped by joy, trust, and purpose Across the four communities, caregivers and families described FFN care as an intentional and dignified choice. Their words and creative work revealed care that protects childhood, carries culture, responds to individual needs, and gives families room to breathe. Care is a source of joy, purpose, identity, and pride Caregivers spoke about the satisfaction of helping children learn, recognize their gifts, and grow into themselves. Many understood caregiving as a calling carried with pride. Care moves across generations Many caregivers traced their practice to the people who raised them, seeing themselves as part of a lineage that passes knowledge, values, and ways of loving forward. Trust creates a distinct form of safety Parents valued caregivers who understood their children's routines, personalities, needs, and identities. That familiarity created peace for children and the adults who depend on their care. Caregivers want connection and recognition Some caregivers described isolation and a desire for spaces to gather, share resources, and build relationships with other FFN families. Families imagine support without losing autonomy Participants envisioned resources, fair compensation, benefits, books, toys, and policies designed around FFN care rather than forcing it to become something else. 40% of participant paintings used green or purple, associated with vitality and growth or royalty and dignity. 43% included blue, reflecting peace, safety, and belonging.   Red and yellow appeared together as expressions of purpose and joy. Orange appeared less often, suggesting pain was present but did not dominate the future participants imagined. Based on NBCDI's Afrofuturist Explorations color analysis, not population-level statistical data. CARE IN THEIR OWN WORDS The people closest to the care know its value “When I'm with my children, I look for their gifts and their qualities, and I cultivate that.” FFN CAREGIVER “My grandma is really my saving grace. I appreciate that she's there so I can have a little break.” FFN CARE PARENT “When they come in and we are caregivers, we want them to have joy. We play and learn as we do everything.” FFN CAREGIVER “Caregiving like this is an extension of home. A child doesn't have only one home. The whole community around that child can be home.” DOCUMENTARY PARTICIPANT THE FUTURE WE IMAGINE What if every family could choose care without compromise Imagine a child care ecosystem that begins with trust in families. Parents can choose the person who is right for their child. Caregivers receive fair compensation and benefits. Books, toys, food, transportation, and learning materials do not have to come from a caregiver's own pocket. FFN caregivers have places to gather, exchange knowledge, and build community without giving up the independence that makes their care distinct. In this future, Black children are surrounded by adults who protect their childhood, affirm who they are, and recognize who they can become. Public systems follow the wisdom already present in families and communities. Research listens differently. Policy makes room for the full ecosystem of care. That future does not begin from nothing. Its foundation is already here, in the care next door. “The future I imagine for Family, Friend, and Neighbor care is one of abundance.” DOCUMENTARY PARTICIPANT What would become possible if the caregivers families already trust had the resources they deserve?
